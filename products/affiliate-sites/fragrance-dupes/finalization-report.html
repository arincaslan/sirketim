The board's verdict was: right work, wrong shape — and it has been acted on. Nine informational guides compete in the most saturated vertical online and convert nobody. The three new pieces are original-anchored and land directly on the fragrance pages that will later hold affiliate links.

The honesty problem, and how it was solved. An “alternatives” piece normally names dupe products — and we have none verified, because the previous listings named products that did not exist. So each piece anchors on the original instead, which we do have real researched data for: it analyses the actual note structure, explains why that original is easy or hard to copy, says what to check before buying, then links to genuinely adjacent originals in the catalog. Baccarat Rouge 540 is six notes built on freely-available amberwood, which is precisely why it is the most copied fragrance of the decade; Aventus is twelve notes whose character is an interaction, and whose own batches vary — so any precise match percentage claims more precision than the target itself supports. Each piece states plainly that we list no alternative products yet, and why. That analysis stays true whichever bottle the reader buys, and needs no rewrite when real listings land.

The payment rail — decided 20 September

A new fact about the market reopened a closed question. August settled this on the premise that every producer was American or European, and the accounting report rejected a Turkish rail on exactly that ground — Dossier and ALT. are US, Divain is ES, and billing them in lira through a Turkish PSP is the wrong shape. The founder has since said revenue comes from the USA, Europe and Türkiye. That is a new premise, not a new opinion, so it reopens the conclusion rather than contradicting it.

The call is one rail: Paddle, paid out to the company Payoneer account that already exists, with annual billing offered and preferred. No Turkish PSP yet. The provider is unchanged from the board’s 10 Sep call. What is new is the payout route and the billing interval — and between them they take the effective cost from roughly 20% to roughly 7% without touching a price or writing a line of adapter. Verified 20 Sep against each provider’s own documentation rather than an aggregator, and written up at departments/accounting/reports/payment-rails-investigation.md §11.

Lever one — the $15 payout fee is avoidable, and the reason is structural

Paddle pays out by wire or by Payoneer, and it pays in 13 currencies of which TRY is not one — so a payout to a Turkish bank is structurally SWIFT and the $15 is not negotiable. It is avoidable instead: Payoneer supplies a US-domiciled USD receiving account, so a USD payout into it is a domestic transfer rather than a cross-border one. Sirketim’s company Payoneer account already exists, opened 30 Aug for CJ. This is the same US-domiciled-versus-USD-denominated distinction that decided the Amazon row at the top of this page.

Per payoutTo a Turkish bank — $15 flatTo Payoneer — ~2%
$130$15 — 11.5%~$2.40 — 1.8%
$300$15 — 5.0%~$5.50 — 1.8%
$750$15 — 2.0%~$14 — 1.8%
$1,500$15 — 1.0%~$28 — 1.8%

Below roughly $750 the Payoneer route wins; above it the wire does — a flat fee and a percentage fee cross over, so this is a per-payout choice and not a permanent one. The $100 minimum applies either way, and Paddle allows the threshold to be raised, so batching is a second and independent lever on the same fee.

Lever two — annual billing, and it needs no pricing decision

On a $9.99 charge Paddle’s 50¢ flat component is 5% by itself, so the headline “5% + 50¢” is a 10% rate at that ticket size. On a $99 charge the same 50¢ is 0.5%. The prices to do something about it are already written: priceYearly: 99 and 179 sit in lib/plans.ts and generate through into the producer Worker as priceYearlyUsd, and BillingInterval is already an enum column on Subscription. Nothing here is a pricing decision — only a billing-interval choice at checkout.

Annual billing does not automatically push us past the crossover, because the crossover is per payout and ten annual subscriptions renewing on ten different dates is still ten small payouts. If renewals ever cluster into one large one, the wire wins again — which is the same point as above, and the reason the route is reviewed rather than fixed.

Türkiye is a legal question before it is a technical one

32 Sayılı Karar bars persons resident in Türkiye from denominating service-contract prices in foreign currency between themselves. There is an exception for contracts covering software produced abroad — the console is produced here, so it does not apply, and billing a Turkish producer directly in USD is the wrong shape. A merchant of record plausibly dissolves this, because the seller of record becomes Paddle, a non-resident, so the contract is no longer between two Turkish residents. That is an argument, not an answer, and it is a mali müşavir question rather than a research one. It will not be settled by reading more.

Nothing is blocked on it today. A Turkish producer is served first by the route that already exists and needs no PSP at all: the A.Ş. invoices a Turkish company and takes a bank transfer. Accounting already records direct-to-customer invoicing as available now with zero setup. Building a second rail for a population of zero would buy a second live credential, a second webhook, a second reconciliation path and an e-fatura obligation, to serve nobody.

Paddle cannot bill in Turkish lira — and a search result said it could. The same aggregated list also named Russian Rubles, which Paddle does not process at all. Checked against Paddle’s own developer documentation: TRY is not a supported payment currency and is not one of the 13 payout currencies either. Recorded because the false version was one sentence from being written here as fact, and what caught it was an unrelated implausibility elsewhere in the same list rather than any check of the claim itself. The consequence for a producer is real either way: a Turkish subscriber on this rail is billed in USD and sees a foreign-currency charge on a Turkish card.

The same decision from the code side

Nothing in the Worker writes a Subscription row, and production holds zero of them — confirmed against the database the deployed Worker actually reads, which is not the one .dev.vars points at. So the Paddle adapter would be the first thing ever to exercise entitlementOf() against a real row, and it would do it through a third party’s webhook, in production, on the day a producer first pays.

The smallest useful first step is therefore not the adapter. It is a manual rail — an admin route that records a subscription a human agreed and a human collected. It serves a Turkish producer today with no PSP at all, it writes the first Subscription row this project has ever had, and it exercises the entitlement path with us holding both ends of it. Every gate it proves is a gate the webhook will not have to prove later, on the worst possible day to be finding out.

A trap that is live right now and would be found by a paying producer. entitlementOf() uppercases status and passes tier through untouched; allowanceForTier() is a case-sensitive switch over "free" / "standard" / "unlimited" with default: "unknown". A webhook that writes the provider’s own plan label — "Standard" — therefore produces a producer who is in force and cannot submit anything: entitled and quota-less at the same time, having just paid. The refuse-to-guess default is correct and should not move. What is missing is that nothing normalises the tier on the way in, and the only writer that will ever supply it is a system whose casing we do not control.

Three schema changes are free today and are data migrations the day after the first row exists. There are zero rows. This is the same window that made the featured→unlimited rename cost nothing on 18 Sep.

ChangeWhy, and why now
@@unique([provider, providerCustomerId])providerCustomerId is globally @unique today, which asserts something no provider guarantees — a customer id is unique within a provider. It also makes a sandbox account, a second provider and the Turkish rail this section defers mutually exclusive with the live one, by constraint rather than by decision.
Period columns to timestamptzcurrentPeriodStart and currentPeriodEnd are Prisma DateTime, which lands as a zoneless timestamp — the exact shape that made every magic-link token born already expired, hidden only by the database defaulting to UTC. The same bug with a subscription attached is a paying producer refused, or a lapsed one served. Note the gate must also compute currentPeriodEnd > now() in SQL, not against a Worker clock.
An idempotency / ordering columnWebhooks retry and arrive out of order. With no stored event id and no sequence, a redelivered cancelled silently overwrites a newer active, and nothing anywhere would show it happened.

The largest risk on this rail is not a fee, and it has not moved since 26 August. Paddle’s acceptable-use policy is reportedly restrictive toward marketplaces, and Counterscent is literally one. The thing being sold is arguably plain SaaS — access to a listing tool — which Paddle does support. But that is our reading of their policy, not their answer, and KYC tightened after Paddle’s 2025 FTC settlement. It has to be asked before an adapter is written, because the failure mode is not a worse rate, it is no rail and a rewrite. 5.2 exists to close this and gates nothing else — it can be sent today.

The integrity problem — reformed 8 September

The formula rewarded a merchant’s copywriter. Two dupe houses, within two days of each other, published the original’s own note pyramid as their product’s — and a formula that is half declared-note overlap read that as a near-perfect composition match. It is not a match; it is marketing copy. One listing’s declared notes were the original’s note for note, scoring 99 raw. The existing defences caught only that extreme end: a copy-detector needing notes and facets to match, and a 90% cap on anything unverified. Scores of 85–89 published untouched.

Fixing it became urgent because of what comes next. The producer subscription (Phase 5) would make outside producers a second and much larger source of exactly this self-reported data — and unlike a merchant, a producer whose revenue depends on rank has a reason to do it deliberately.

Four changes shipped as one commit, because each moves scores and staging them would mean explaining three different numbers to the same reader:

ChangeWhat it does, and what it measured
95% structural ceilingApplies to every published score, including editorially verified ones, which were previously uncapped to 100. Two fragrances can declare identical notes and still not contain them in the same proportions — a note list says what went in, never how much — so no number built on declared notes should claim otherwise.
−10 where the pyramid is oursSome merchants publish one flat note list and no tiers at all, so we decide which notes are top, heart and base — and that choice alone can move a score 12 points. 47 of 79 listings are in this position. The penalty prices our own uncertainty rather than pretending it away, and the badge says so on the page.
Family bug fixedThe family term had been hardcoded to full credit since the formula was written. A no-op for all 79 current listings by construction — every one shares its reference’s family — so it only starts discriminating for a future cross-family listing.
Ingredient overlap, 15%A fourth component comparing published INCI lists as one flat set. Inert until both sides have a list, and none do yet — a missing list is never scored as a mismatch. Backfilling it across the catalog (~144 lookups) is the open follow-on.

One exception, and it is human rather than formulaic. The founder may set a listing’s score personally — the only way past 95 — with a written justification shown on the page, its own badge (never conflated with “editorially verified”: one is an independent check, the other is one person’s disclosed opinion), no use on our own fragrance line, and no power to rescue a listing flagged as a copy. A build-time guard enforces all three. No listing currently uses it.

Measured against the shipped functions, before and after. The 32 listings whose sellers publish real pyramids moved by exactly zero. Of the 47 whose split was ours, 41 fell exactly 10 and six fell less because they already sat at the cap. One ranking changed anywhere on the site: on Aventus, the listing this project had already flagged as suspiciously #1 dropped to #3, behind two whose sellers publish their own tiering. That is the penalty doing precisely the job it was added for.

× What is still not solved, stated plainly. The penalty prices a missing pyramid, not copying. A merchant who copies the original’s pyramid and publishes it as three proper tiers is still scored at face value, and the copy-detector — which needs the facets to match too, and we author those — remains the only defence against that case. And the older data-authorship problem is unchanged and unfixable in code: a listing whose declared notes are written to sit close to the reference legitimately out-ranks honest ones, because the formula only ever sees the data it is given. That is why there are still no house products, and why adding one is a board decision rather than a code change.
