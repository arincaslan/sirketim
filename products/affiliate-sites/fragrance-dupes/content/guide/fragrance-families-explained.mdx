Most people discover fragrances one bottle at a time, liking some and not
others, without ever spotting the pattern underneath. Families are that
pattern. Learn which one you gravitate to and the search collapses from
"thousands of bottles" to "maybe forty worth smelling."

It is also the fastest way to find a good alternative to something expensive:
a dupe that shares the family will feel related even where the note list
differs, while one that matches notes across a family boundary usually won't.

## The four classical families, and the honest fifth

The traditional fragrance wheel has four groups. Modern perfumery has largely
outgrown it, but it is still the most useful starting frame.

### Fresh

Citrus, aquatic, green, and aromatic. Bergamot, lemon, grapefruit, sea notes,
mint, basil, lavender.

**Reads as:** clean, bright, awake. The default "smells nice and offends
nobody" territory, which is both its strength and its limitation.

**Trade-off worth knowing:** fresh materials are volatile by nature, so this
family is consistently the shortest-lived. That is chemistry, not corner-cutting
— see <a href="/guide/how-to-make-a-fragrance-last-longer">how to make a
fragrance last longer</a>.

Examples in our catalog: <a href="/fragrance/acqua-di-gio">Acqua di Gio</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/eau-sauvage">Eau Sauvage</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/silver-mountain-water">Silver Mountain Water</a>.

### Floral

Rose, jasmine, tuberose, iris, orange blossom, lily of the valley. The largest
family by a wide margin, and the most varied — a sharp green floral and a
creamy white floral have little in common beyond the label.

**Reads as:** anything from crisp and soapy to heavy and narcotic.

Examples: <a href="/fragrance/chanel-no-5">Chanel No. 5</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/jadore">J'adore</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/gucci-bloom">Gucci Bloom</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/miss-dior">Miss Dior</a>.

### Oriental (increasingly called *amber*)

Vanilla, amber, resins, incense, warm spices, balsams.

**Reads as:** warm, sweet, enveloping, often heavy. Excellent longevity,
because these materials are among the least volatile.

The name is shifting — many houses and databases now say **amber** instead,
partly because "oriental" is an exoticising term inherited from the 19th
century. Same family, better word.

Examples: <a href="/fragrance/tobacco-vanille">Tobacco Vanille</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/baccarat-rouge-540">Baccarat Rouge 540</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/hypnotic-poison">Hypnotic Poison</a>.

### Woody

Sandalwood, cedar, vetiver, oud, patchouli.

**Reads as:** dry, warm, grounded. Often the backbone under another family
rather than the headline.

Examples: <a href="/fragrance/santal-33">Santal 33</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/sycomore">Sycomore</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/oud-ispahan">Oud Ispahan</a>.

### The fifth: fougère and chypre

Two *structures* rather than ingredient groups, and between them they account
for a huge share of men's fragrance in particular.

- **Fougère** — lavender, coumarin, oakmoss. The classic "barbershop" accord.
  <a href="/fragrance/le-male">Le Male</a> sits here.
- **Chypre** — bergamot over oakmoss and labdanum. Sophisticated, slightly
  bitter, out of fashion and quietly returning. Reformulation hit this family
  hardest, because oakmoss is now restricted for allergen reasons.

If a fragrance feels "classic" in a way you cannot name, it is usually one of
these two.

## Why family beats notes when you are comparing

A note list tells you what went in. A family tells you what comes out — and
the second is what you actually experience.

Two fragrances can share ten notes and belong to different families because the
**proportions** differ, and they will not smell alike. That is the single most
common reason a dupe with a near-identical pyramid disappoints. We cover the
mechanics in <a href="/guide/how-to-read-a-note-pyramid">how to read a note
pyramid</a>.

This is why our <a href="/dupe-finder">dupe finder</a> scores **facets**
alongside note overlap — sweet, fresh, woody, spicy, powdery. Facets survive
the proportion problem in a way ingredient lists don't, and the
<a href="/about#methodology">published formula</a> weights them at 35%
precisely for that reason.

## How to find your own family in one afternoon

Skip the quizzes. Work backwards from what you already own or have liked.

1. **List three fragrances you genuinely enjoyed** — including ones you smelled
   on somebody else.
2. **Look up each one's family** on its page in our
   <a href="/library">reference library</a>.
3. **Look for the overlap.** Three out of three in one family is a clear
   answer. Two out of three still narrows things enormously.
4. **Check the facets, not just the family name.** If all three are floral but
   all three are also *powdery*, the powder is doing more work than the flowers.

Then shop that family first. You will still find exceptions you love — but you
will stop buying bottles you already knew, on some level, that you wouldn't
wear.

## A caveat worth stating

Families are a map, not the territory. Modern perfumery deliberately crosses
lines: a "fresh woody amber" is a genuine and common description, not a hedge.
<a href="/fragrance/baccarat-rouge-540">Baccarat Rouge 540</a> gets filed under
amber, woody, and floral depending on who is filing it, and none of those is
wrong.

Use families to narrow the field and to explain your own taste to yourself. Do
not use them to rule things out before smelling them.
