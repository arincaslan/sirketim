You read the reviews, everyone agreed it was a warm vanilla-amber, you bought
it, and on you it is sharp and vaguely soapy for an hour before settling into
something you were not promised. The usual explanation offered is "skin
chemistry", which is true in the way that "physics" explains why a car will
not start.

Skin chemistry is real, but it is one item on a list, and it is not the item
most likely to be responsible for your specific disappointment. Here is the
list, roughly in order of how often each one is actually the culprit.

## 1. You are smelling it at a different moment than the reviewer

This is the most common and least discussed reason.

A fragrance is not one smell. It is a sequence, and the reviewer whose
description you read may have been writing about a different point in that
sequence than the one you just experienced. Most published impressions
describe the fragrance somewhere between fifteen minutes and two hours in.
Most first impressions in a shop happen in the first ninety seconds, when
you are largely smelling alcohol and the most volatile top notes flashing off.

If you spray and immediately think "this is nothing like they said", you are
frequently right — and it will still become what they said in twenty minutes.
Our <a href="/guide/how-to-find-a-fragrance-dupe-that-works">guide to picking
a dupe</a> makes the same point from the other direction: decide which part
of the wear you actually care about before you shop.

## 2. Nose fatigue, which happens faster than you think

Olfactory adaptation is aggressive. Your nose stops registering a continuous
smell within minutes — this is why you cannot smell your own home but visitors
can. Applied to fragrance, two consequences follow:

- **You will lose the ability to smell your own fragrance long before other
  people do.** "It disappeared after an hour" very often means "I stopped
  noticing it after an hour." Ask somebody else before concluding a fragrance
  has no longevity.
- **Testing more than three or four fragrances in one session is close to
  pointless.** By the fifth, you are not evaluating, you are guessing. Coffee
  beans do not reset this, despite the ritual in every department store —
  what actually helps is smelling your own skin or sleeve, or simply
  stopping.

## 3. Concentration and formula differences between versions

The bottle you bought may not be the bottle the reviewer reviewed.

The same name is routinely sold in several concentrations, and those are
usually **different formulas**, not one liquid at different dilutions. A
review of the extrait tells you comparatively little about the eau de
toilette. We cover this in detail in
<a href="/guide/fragrance-concentrations-explained">what fragrance
concentration actually changes</a>, and it is worth checking before you
conclude your skin is the problem.

Reformulation compounds this. Long-lived fragrances get reworked over the
years — sometimes for cost, often because a material has been restricted for
safety or supply reasons. A review written in 2014 may be describing a
composition that no longer exists.
<a href="/fragrance/fahrenheit">Fahrenheit</a>,
<a href="/fragrance/eau-sauvage">Eau Sauvage</a> and several of the older
<a href="/fragrance/chanel-no-5">Chanel</a> classics have all been through
this. If a fragrance has a devoted following insisting "the old one was
better", that is usually what they mean.

## 4. Now, actual skin chemistry

Having put it fourth rather than first, it does matter. The main levers:

**Skin pH and sebum.** Oilier skin holds aromatic molecules longer, which is
why the same fragrance frequently lasts noticeably longer on some people. Dry
skin releases a fragrance faster — the composition unfolds quicker and
finishes sooner.

**Your own scent.** Everyone has a baseline body odour that is not unpleasant
and is not neutral either. A fragrance layers over it, not onto a blank
surface. This is genuinely why a musky or ambery base can read differently
person to person — those materials sit closest to the skin and blend most
with it.

**Diet, medication and hormones** shift all of the above, which is why a
fragrance can behave differently on you in different months.

What this does *not* usually do is transform one fragrance into a completely
different one. Skin chemistry shifts emphasis and duration. It rarely turns a
warm amber into a soapy aquatic. If that is what you experienced, look back at
reasons one to three.

## 5. Where you sprayed it, and onto what

- **Skin versus paper.** A blotter shows you the composition without your
  skin's contribution and without body heat driving evaporation. It is a good
  way to compare two fragrances against each other and a poor way to predict
  how one will wear on you.
- **Skin versus fabric.** Fragrance lasts dramatically longer on clothing —
  sometimes for days — because there is no heat, no sebum and no absorption.
  It also develops far less. If you want to smell the fragrance as composed,
  spray skin; if you want longevity, fabric will win and flatten it.
- **Pulse points.** The conventional advice to spray wrists and neck is about
  warmth increasing projection. It works, and it also accelerates the whole
  arc. Spraying somewhere cooler (chest under a shirt) will project less and
  last longer.
- **Rubbing your wrists together.** This is the one piece of folk wisdom
  worth actually abandoning. It crushes the top notes and speeds up the
  opening, so you skip part of what you paid for.

## 6. Environment

Heat increases projection substantially — the same fragrance is a different
proposition in July than in November. Humidity carries scent further. Cold,
dry air suppresses it, which is why a heavy winter fragrance can seem to be
doing nothing outdoors at 2°C and then become overwhelming the moment you
step into a warm room.

## How to work out which one it is

A short diagnostic that will resolve most cases:

1. **Spray once on skin, then leave it alone for thirty minutes.** Do not
   evaluate it in the first five.
2. **Ask somebody else** whether they can still smell it at the four-hour
   mark, before deciding it has no longevity.
3. **Check the concentration** on the bottle you own against the one being
   reviewed.
4. **Try it on a blotter alongside** the same fragrance on your skin. If they
   smell substantially different, that is your skin. If they smell the same
   and both differ from the reviews, it is the version, the batch, or the
   review.
5. **Retest on a different day.** Once is not data.
