A designer fragrance costs €80. A niche one costs €250. An alternative to
either costs €30. The liquid inside is not five or eight times better in the
second case, and it is not eight times worse in the third.

Here is what the money is actually buying, so you can decide which parts you
care about.

## Where the money goes in a designer bottle

For a mainstream designer fragrance at €80–120, the juice is a small
proportion of the price. The rest is roughly:

- **Marketing and celebrity contracts** — the single largest line for major
  launches. A campaign face can cost millions.
- **Retail margin** — department stores take a substantial cut, and the price
  has to survive frequent discounting.
- **Packaging** — heavy glass, magnetic caps, cellophane, the box inside the
  box.
- **Brand licensing** — most fashion houses license their name to a fragrance
  conglomerate rather than making it themselves.
- **The formula** — real, and usually the smallest share.

None of this is a scandal. It is how consumer goods work, and the same
breakdown applies to trainers and sunglasses. But it explains why a similar
scent can exist at a fraction of the price without anyone being cheated.

## What niche genuinely does differently

Niche is not just "designer with a bigger price tag," and it is worth being
fair about what changes.

**Materials budget.** A niche house selling 5,000 bottles a year can afford
per-bottle material costs a house selling five million cannot. Real oud, high
grades of iris, natural sandalwood — these are genuinely expensive and show up
in the composition.

**Fewer commercial constraints.** A designer launch has to be broadly likeable
because it must sell at scale. A niche house can make something divisive on
purpose. That is why niche fragrances are more often *interesting* — and also
why more of them are unwearable for any given person.

**Concentration and longevity** are frequently higher, though not universally.

**What niche also buys**, honestly: exclusivity and a story. Some of the price
is simply that not many people have it. Whether that is worth €150 is a
personal question, not a technical one.

<a href="/fragrance/baccarat-rouge-540">Baccarat Rouge 540</a> and
<a href="/fragrance/aventus">Aventus</a> are useful examples: both genuinely
distinctive compositions, both also enormously duped precisely *because* they
are distinctive and expensive.

## What a dupe gets you, and what it doesn't

**Gets you:** the shape of the scent. A competent alternative reproduces the
dominant accord and the general impression convincingly, especially in the
first hour, and often at 10–20% of the price.

**Usually doesn't get you:**

- **The base.** This is where alternatives most often thin out, and it is the
  part you and everyone around you spend the most time with. Check base overlap
  first — see <a href="/guide/how-to-read-a-note-pyramid">how to read a note
  pyramid</a>.
- **Longevity**, often because concentration is lower. See
  <a href="/guide/fragrance-concentrations-explained">what concentration
  actually changes</a>.
- **The expensive naturals**, swapped for synthetics. Not automatically worse —
  much of modern perfumery is synthetic by choice, and synthetics are often more
  stable and more ethical — but it can flatten a composition's detail.
- **Resale, packaging, and the object itself**, if those matter to you.

Dupes are also legal, which surprises people. Formulas are not copyrightable;
names, logos and bottle shapes are. That distinction is the whole difference
between a dupe and a counterfeit — see
<a href="/guide/are-fragrance-dupes-legal-and-safe">are fragrance dupes
legal</a>.

## A sensible way to spend

Rather than picking a tier and staying in it:

**Buy the original when** the fragrance is genuinely singular and you wear it
constantly. Some compositions have no close alternative, and a signature scent
you wear daily for years is a low cost per wear.

**Buy the alternative when** you want variety, you are trying a family you do
not know yet, or you want something for a context where you would rather not
wear a €250 bottle. This is most of the time, for most people.

**Buy a decant of either** before committing to 100ml of anything. The single
most effective way to stop wasting money on fragrance is to stop buying full
bottles of things you have not worn for a full day.

## How to judge a specific comparison

Rather than trusting a percentage:

1. **Compare the bases**, not the top notes. The opening is what sells; the
   base is what you live with.
2. **Compare the facets** — sweet, fresh, woody, spicy, powdery — which describe
   how it reads rather than what went in.
3. **Check the concentration of both.**
4. **Compare price per millilitre**, not price per bottle.
