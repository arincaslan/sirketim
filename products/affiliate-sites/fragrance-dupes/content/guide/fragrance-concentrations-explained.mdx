The labels on a fragrance box look like a strength dial: eau de cologne at the
bottom, eau de toilette above it, eau de parfum above that, extrait de parfum
at the top. Buy the strongest one and you get more of the same thing, only
louder and for longer.

That is not how it works, and the gap between that assumption and reality is
responsible for a lot of disappointed purchases — including a lot of
disappointed *dupe* purchases, because concentration is one of the two or
three things most likely to explain why something that "matches the notes"
still smells wrong to you.

## There is no legal definition of any of these terms

Start here, because everything else follows from it. In most markets,
including the US and the EU, **no regulation defines what percentage of
aromatic compound makes something an eau de parfum rather than an eau de
toilette.** The terms are marketing conventions, not standards.

The commonly quoted ranges are roughly:

- **Eau de cologne** — around 2–5% aromatic compound
- **Eau de toilette** — around 5–15%
- **Eau de parfum** — around 15–20%
- **Extrait de parfum** (also "parfum", "pure parfum") — around 20–40%

Treat those as a description of typical practice, not a specification. A
house can label a 12% juice an eau de parfum and nobody can stop them. Two
different brands' EDPs can differ more from each other than one brand's EDT
differs from its own EDP.

This matters commercially: **concentration is not a reliable proxy for
quality, longevity or value.** It is a rough signal, and rough signals are
exactly what get exploited in a category where the buyer cannot smell the
product before paying.

## The bigger secret: the formula usually changes too

Here is the part that surprises people. When a house releases an EDT and an
EDP of the same fragrance, it is usually **not the same liquid at two
dilutions.** It is frequently a reworked formula — different proportions,
sometimes different materials entirely.

That is a deliberate choice, and often a sensible one. A material that is
pleasant at low concentration can become harsh or cloying when you simply
scale it up. Perfumers rebalance rather than multiply. The practical
consequence is that the EDP version of a fragrance you love can smell
noticeably different, not merely stronger — and occasionally you will prefer
the "weaker" one.

<a href="/fragrance/dior-homme">Dior Homme</a> and
<a href="/fragrance/dior-homme-intense">Dior Homme Intense</a> are a useful
pair to think about here, as are
<a href="/fragrance/sauvage">Sauvage</a> and
<a href="/fragrance/sauvage-elixir">Sauvage Elixir</a>. These are marketed as
intensity steps within a line, but they are separate compositions with their
own character, which is why people who love one are sometimes indifferent to
the other.

So the honest advice is: **do not buy the higher concentration of something
you have never smelled on the assumption that it is a safer version of a
fragrance you already like.** It is a different product wearing the same name.

## What concentration actually buys you

Setting the formula question aside, higher concentration genuinely tends to
deliver two things:

**Longevity.** More aromatic material means more of it left on skin after
several hours. This is the most consistent, most predictable difference.

**A slower opening.** Higher concentrations often feel less sharp in the
first ten minutes, because there is proportionally less alcohol flashing off.
The fragrance arrives rather than announcing itself.

What it does *not* reliably buy you is **projection** — how far the scent
carries. Projection is driven much more by which materials are in the
formula than by how much total material is present. Plenty of EDTs project
harder than plenty of extraits. A big citrus-and-aromatic opening will fill a
room at 8% while a dense, resinous extrait sits close to the skin at 30%.

If what you want is to be noticed across a table, you are shopping for a
composition, not a concentration.

## Why this is the first thing to check on a dupe

An alternative that reproduces a fragrance's note structure but sits at a
lower concentration will usually wear lighter and fade sooner than the
original. That is not the producer cutting corners — extrait is genuinely
more expensive to make, and a lower-concentration product at a lower price is
a legitimate trade, honestly described.

The problem is that it is frequently **not** honestly described. A product
page will list a note pyramid that matches an extrait original, quote a price
a fraction of it, and never state its own concentration anywhere. If you are
comparing on notes alone, that looks like an extraordinary deal. It usually
is not.

So before you compare anything else:

1. **Find the original's concentration.** If a fragrance exists in several,
   identify which one the comparison is actually against.
2. **Find the alternative's concentration.** If the seller does not state it
   anywhere, treat that as information in itself.
3. **Compare price per millilitre, not price per bottle.** A 30ml at $29 and
   a 100ml at $75 are not the price relationship they first appear to be.

## When the lighter version is the right buy

Concentration is not a ladder you should always climb. Reasons to
deliberately choose the lighter option:

- **You want it for daytime, indoors, around other people.** An extrait in a
  small meeting room is an imposition, not a flex.
- **Warm weather.** Heat amplifies projection substantially. A fragrance that
  is well-judged in November can be overwhelming in July.
- **You are still deciding whether you like it.** A smaller, cheaper, lighter
  version is a much better way to find out than committing to 100ml of
  extrait.
- **You prefer the composition.** Sometimes the EDT simply is the better
  fragrance. <a href="/fragrance/eau-sauvage">Eau Sauvage</a> has a lot of
  devoted wearers who have no interest in a more concentrated interpretation
  of it.

## The one-line version

Concentration reliably predicts **how long** a fragrance lasts. It does not
reliably predict **how good it is, how far it projects, or how much it should
cost** — and it very often does not predict **what it smells like**, because
the formula behind the label frequently changed too.

Check it first when you are comparing a dupe to an original, then stop
treating it as a ranking.
