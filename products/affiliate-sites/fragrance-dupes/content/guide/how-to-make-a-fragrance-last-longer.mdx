"It disappears after two hours" is the single most common complaint in
fragrance, and it has four completely different causes. Fixing the wrong one is
why so much advice fails.

Work through these in order. The first two are free and account for most cases.

## First: are you sure it's gone?

Your nose adapts to a continuous smell within minutes — the same reason you
cannot smell your own home. **You will lose the ability to detect your own
fragrance long before anyone else does.**

So before treating this as a longevity problem, get one outside data point.
Ask someone at the four-hour mark, or leave the room for fifteen minutes and
come back. A surprising share of "it vanished" turns out to be "I stopped
noticing it," and no amount of technique will fix a perception issue.

Sniffing your own sleeve is the cheapest test: fabric holds fragrance far
longer than skin, so if the sleeve still smells right, the juice is fine.

## Second: where you spray, and onto what

This is where the real gains are.

**Moisturise first.** Dry skin is the most common genuine cause of poor
longevity. Aromatic molecules need something to cling to; dry skin lets them
evaporate faster. An unscented moisturiser or a plain balm on the spray points,
applied just before, is the single most effective free fix — often worth an
extra hour or two. Scented lotion works against you by adding a competing
smell.

**Spray onto skin, not into the air.** Walking through a cloud puts most of the
fragrance on the floor. Two or three direct sprays beat six atomised ones.

**Pick cooler spots for longevity, warmer for projection.** Wrists and neck are
warm, so they push scent outward and burn through it faster. Chest under a
shirt, the back of the neck, or behind the ears will last noticeably longer
while projecting less. Choose based on what you actually want.

**Do not rub your wrists together.** This is the one piece of folk wisdom worth
abandoning outright. It crushes the top notes and accelerates the opening, so
you lose part of what you paid for and gain nothing.

**Use fabric deliberately.** Fragrance can last for days on a scarf, a collar,
or a jacket lining, because there is no body heat and no absorption. The
trade-off is that it develops much less — you get a flatter, more static
version. Spray skin for the fragrance as composed, fabric when you want it to
persist. Test on an inconspicuous spot first; some oils stain, especially on
silk and pale fabrics.

**Hair holds fragrance well** but alcohol dries it out. If you do it, spray a
brush rather than your hair directly.

## Third: is the bottle actually built to last?

If the technique is right and it still fades, the product may simply not be
capable of more, and no amount of ritual changes that.

Two things to check:

**Concentration.** An eau de toilette will generally fade sooner than an eau de
parfum of the same fragrance — though they are usually different formulas, not
one at two strengths, which is worth understanding before you upgrade blindly.
See <a href="/guide/fragrance-concentrations-explained">what concentration
actually changes</a>.

**What family it's in.** This matters more than most people expect, and it is
not a quality issue:

- **Citrus and fresh aromatics are volatile by nature.** A bright bergamot
  opening is *chemically* short-lived. <a href="/fragrance/eau-sauvage">Eau
  Sauvage</a> and <a href="/fragrance/acqua-di-gio">Acqua di Gio</a> are not
  poorly made because they fade; that is what those materials do.
- **Woods, resins, ambers and musks last for hours** because the molecules are
  heavier and evaporate slowly. <a href="/fragrance/tobacco-vanille">Tobacco
  Vanille</a> or <a href="/fragrance/baccarat-rouge-540">Baccarat Rouge 540</a>
  persist because of what they are made of.

**If you want longevity, shop the base, not the brand.** A cheap ambery-woody
fragrance will usually outlast an expensive citrus one. Our
<a href="/library">reference library</a> lists the note pyramid and profile for
every original we cover, so you can check what a fragrance is built on before
buying — and <a href="/guide/how-to-read-a-note-pyramid">how to read a note
pyramid</a> explains why the base is the part that decides this.

## Fourth: storage, which quietly ruins bottles

Fragrance degrades with **light, heat, and temperature swings** — which makes a
bathroom windowsill close to the worst possible place, and the bathroom in
general a bad room for it.

- Keep bottles in a box or a drawer, somewhere with a stable temperature.
- Leave the juice in its original bottle. Decanting introduces air.
- A largely empty bottle oxidises faster, because what is left is mostly air.
  Finish old ones rather than keeping twenty at 10%.

A fragrance that has turned smells sharp or sour in the opening and thin
afterwards. That is not fixable.

## What does not work

Cutting through the folklore:

- **Vaseline on pulse points.** Marginal at best, and a plain moisturiser does
  the same job without the residue.
- **Spraying your clothes to "make it stronger."** It makes it *last longer*
  and smell flatter. Different outcome.
- **Buying the "intense" or "extrait" version blind.** Often a different
  composition, not a stronger one — you may not like it.
- **More sprays.** Past three or four you mostly stop noticing while everyone
  else notices a great deal. Overapplication is the most common fragrance
  mistake and the hardest to self-diagnose, for the adaptation reason above.
- **Coffee beans between testers.** A shop ritual with little evidence behind
  it. What actually resets you is stopping, or smelling your own skin.

## The short version

Moisturise first, spray directly onto skin, choose cooler spots if you want
duration and warmer ones if you want reach, never rub, and store bottles away
from light and heat. Then check whether you bought a citrus — because if you
did, four hours is the product working correctly, not failing.
