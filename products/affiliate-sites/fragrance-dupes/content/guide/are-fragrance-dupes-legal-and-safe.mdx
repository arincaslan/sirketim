Two questions come up constantly, and they get tangled together even though
they are unrelated. One is about law, the other about what is in the bottle.

## Dupes are legal. Counterfeits are not.

The distinction is not a technicality — it decides everything about what you
are buying.

**A fragrance formula is generally not protected by copyright or patent.**
Courts in most jurisdictions have declined to treat a scent as a copyrightable
work, and the industry protects formulas as **trade secrets** instead. So a
company that analyses a popular fragrance and creates something that smells
similar is, in principle, doing something lawful — the same way a supermarket
can sell a cola that tastes like a famous one.

**What *is* protected is everything around the scent:** the brand name, the
logo, the bottle shape, and the packaging design. Bottle shapes in particular
are often registered as trade dress or design rights, which is why dupe houses
sell in plain, generic flacons.

So the line is:

| | Legal dupe | Counterfeit |
|---|---|---|
| Sold as | Its **own** brand name | The **original's** name and logo |
| Bottle | Generic, its own design | Copies the original's shape and label |
| Claim | "Inspired by" / "our take on" | Pretends to *be* the original |
| Buyer knows | Yes | No — that is the point |

A counterfeit is trademark infringement and, in many places, a criminal
offence. It is also the version that carries real safety risk, for reasons
below.

**A practical tell:** a legitimate dupe house names the original in comparison
copy but never on the bottle, and never uses its logo. If the packaging is
trying to look like the designer's packaging, you are not looking at a dupe.

**Buying either for personal use is legal essentially everywhere.** The legal
exposure sits with sellers, not with you. Where buyers do get caught out is
customs: counterfeits can be seized and destroyed at the border, and you
generally do not get your money back.

## The safety question is separate — and it is about the seller, not the price

"Cheap" and "unsafe" are not the same thing. A €30 fragrance from a real
company operating under cosmetics regulation is not more dangerous than a €300
one; both are made from largely the same palette of materials, often by the
same handful of fragrance houses that supply the industry.

What actually matters is whether anyone is accountable for the contents.

**In the EU and UK**, cosmetics — including fragrance — must have a
**responsible person**, a safety assessment, and an ingredient list, and must
respect **IFRA** restrictions on materials that are sensitising or otherwise
limited. In the US, fragrance is regulated more lightly, though the **MoCRA**
reforms added facility registration and adverse-event reporting.

None of that reaches an anonymous seller shipping unlabelled bottles.

**What to look for before buying from an unfamiliar brand:**

- **A full INCI ingredient list.** Its absence on a cosmetic product is the
  clearest single warning sign.
- **Allergen declarations.** The EU requires named fragrance allergens (linalool,
  limonene, citronellol and others) to be listed. Seeing them is a sign of
  compliance, not of a problem.
- **A real company identity** — a registered address, not just a social account.
- **Batch code and expiry/PAO marking.**
- **Where it ships from**, and whether returns exist.

**What is a genuine risk, and what isn't:**

- Counterfeits are the real hazard. Because nobody is accountable, seized fakes
  have repeatedly been found to contain contaminants and unlisted materials.
  This is a counterfeiting problem, not a dupe problem.
- **Sensitisation is the common real-world issue** even with legitimate
  products. Fragrance allergy is not rare, it can develop over time to
  something you previously wore happily, and it is why patch-testing a new
  fragrance on a small area is sensible regardless of price.
- Cheap does not mean adulterated. It usually means cheaper materials, less
  marketing, lower concentration, or a simpler formula — all of which show up
  as a thinner, shorter-lived scent rather than a hazard.

## What "cheaper" actually buys you

Since the formula is not the protected part, the price gap is mostly not about
the liquid. It is packaging, advertising, retail margin, brand licensing, and
the celebrity contract. That is a real reason a similar smell can cost a
fraction as much.

But some of the gap *is* the liquid, and honest comparison means saying so:

- **Concentration is often lower**, which shows up as shorter wear — see
  <a href="/guide/fragrance-concentrations-explained">what concentration
  actually changes</a>.
- **Expensive naturals may be swapped for synthetics.** Not automatically
  worse — much of modern perfumery is synthetic by choice, and synthetics are
  frequently more stable and more ethical — but it can flatten a composition.
- **The structure may be simplified**, matching the impression without the
  detail. This is why note lists look closer than the fragrances smell, which
  <a href="/guide/how-to-read-a-note-pyramid">reading a note pyramid</a>
  explains.
