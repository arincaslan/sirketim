Every fragrance listing shows you a pyramid: top notes, heart notes, base
notes. It looks like a specification. It is closer to a menu description —
useful, written to sell, and not always an accurate account of what is in the
bottle.

Learning to read one properly is the single most transferable skill in
fragrance shopping, and it is the difference between "this dupe lists the same
notes" and "this dupe will actually satisfy me."

## What the three tiers actually describe

The pyramid is not a list of ingredients. It is a rough **timeline**, based on
how quickly different materials evaporate.

**Top notes** are the most volatile — they reach you first and are usually
gone within five to twenty minutes. Citrus, light aromatics, some fresh
spices. They are what you smell in the shop and almost never what you smell
at dinner.

**Heart notes** (or "middle notes") emerge as the top fades and typically run
from around twenty minutes to two or three hours. Florals, fuller spices, tea,
fruit. This is the body of most fragrances and where a composition's character
usually lives.

**Base notes** are the least volatile and the longest-lived: woods, resins,
musks, vanilla, amber, oud, leather. They can persist for many hours and are
what other people associate with you wearing something. The stretch where the
base dominates is called the **drydown**.

The tiers overlap heavily. Nothing switches off at a boundary. A good
composition is designed so the transitions are continuous rather than
stepped — and a poorly built one is exactly where you notice the seams.

## Why base notes deserve most of your attention

If you are comparing an alternative to an original, the base is where the
comparison is won or lost, for a simple reason: **it is where you and everyone
around you will spend the most time.**

A fragrance that nails a bright bergamot-and-pink-pepper opening and then
collapses into a generic sweet musk has matched the twenty minutes you notice
least. One that gets the base right and approximates the opening will feel
much closer to the original over a normal day, even if the first impression is
slightly off.

This is why our matching formula weights the three tiers unequally —
**base notes at 45%, heart at 35%, top at 20%** — rather than counting note
overlap as a flat list. You can see the full method on
<a href="/about#methodology">our methodology page</a>, and the breakdown per
comparison in the <a href="/dupe-finder">dupe finder</a>.

It also explains a frequent complaint that sounds like a contradiction:
"it smells identical for the first ten minutes and then nothing like it." That
is a dupe built to pass the shop-counter test.

## The parts of a pyramid that are not technical

Several things are worth knowing before you treat any pyramid as fact.

**Many listed notes are not ingredients.** "Sea breeze", "rain", "warm skin"
and "night air" are impressions produced by combinations of synthetic
materials. They describe an intended effect. That is legitimate perfumery
language, but you cannot compare it between brands the way you can compare
"bergamot".

**Some real materials are effectively never present.** Natural musk is not
used. Genuine oud is extraordinarily expensive and appears in a small
fraction of the fragrances that list "oud". Ambergris in most listings means
an amber accord. None of this is fraud — it is convention — but it means two
fragrances listing "oud" may have nothing material in common.

**Pyramids are sometimes incomplete or wrong.** Houses are not obliged to
publish a full or accurate breakdown, and they sometimes omit a material that
is doing significant work, or list one for its associations. Fragrance
databases assemble their pyramids from press material, and errors propagate.

**A note's presence tells you nothing about its quantity.** A fragrance can
list twelve notes and be dominated by one. Two fragrances can share ten of
twelve listed notes and smell substantially different because the proportions
differ. This is the single biggest reason note-overlap percentages should be
read as a starting filter, not a verdict.

## How to use a pyramid when comparing

A practical sequence:

1. **Read the base first.** Ignore the top for a moment. If the bases have
   little in common, the two fragrances will not feel alike no matter how
   well the openings match.
2. **Look for the dominant material, not the longest list.** Ask what the
   fragrance is *about*. <a href="/fragrance/tobacco-vanille">Tobacco
   Vanille</a> is about tobacco and vanilla; the rest is scaffolding. If an
   alternative reproduces the scaffolding and thins the centre, the list will
   look close and the fragrance will not be.
3. **Check the facets, not just the notes.** Sweet, fresh, woody, spicy,
   powdery — how a fragrance *reads* is often a better comparison axis than
   its ingredient list, because it survives the proportion problem above.
4. **Discount impressionistic notes.** If two pyramids agree mainly on
   "sea breeze" and "clean linen", they agree on very little.
5. **Then check concentration**, which changes how the whole pyramid
   projects and lasts — see
   <a href="/guide/fragrance-concentrations-explained">our concentration
   guide</a>.

## A worked example of the trap

Consider two fragrances that both list bergamot, jasmine, saffron, cedar and
amber. On paper that is a strong match.

In practice, one might be a saffron-forward composition where jasmine is a
whisper, and the other a jasmine soliflore with a pinch of saffron for
warmth. Same five notes, same tiers, two fragrances nobody would confuse. The
list was never going to tell you that.

This is exactly why we publish facet ratings alongside note overlap, and why a
high note-match score alone is not treated as sufficient. It is also why the
displayed match score on this site is capped for listings we have not been
able to independently verify — a producer who simply copies an original's
published note pyramid into their own listing would otherwise score a perfect
match, having demonstrated nothing at all.

## Quick reference

| Tier | Typical duration | What lives there | Weight when comparing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | 5–20 minutes | Citrus, light aromatics, fresh spice | Lowest |
| Heart | 20 min – 3 hours | Florals, fruit, fuller spice, tea | Medium |
| Base | 3 hours+ | Woods, resins, musk, vanilla, amber, leather | **Highest** |

Read the base, find the dominant material, check the facets, then look at the
percentage. In that order.
